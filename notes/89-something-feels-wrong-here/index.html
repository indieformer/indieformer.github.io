Where most of my time goes

Localisation, store pages and outreach all get done at Indieformer, and they take time. Most of our week goes somewhere else though. We play the games we've signed, over and over, and try to work out why bits of them feel off.

That covers pretty much everything. How it plays, how it looks and sounds, the pacing, whether a cutscene makes sense, whether the story is clear, what the menu looks like.

Something feels wrong here

It usually starts with a small reaction while we're playing. A section drags, or we put the controller down earlier than we thought we would.

We can't name it straight away. So we sit with it, sometimes for a long time, and try to work out what we're feeling and what's causing it. Is the pacing off, or has the game set us up to expect something it isn't giving me? Once we can name it, we can take it to the developer as something specific they can change.

Players notice this stuff all the time. They'll say a game feels samey or slow and leave it there. Working out which part of the game is causing it, and what to change, falls to the developer, and by the time a game is nearly done they've played it so many times they can't feel it the way a new player would.

Where it comes from

Before Indieformer, Josh worked in film and TV as an editor and producer, and he still coaches.

In editing it's called giving notes. You watch a cut as if you've never seen the footage, notice where your attention drops or something stops making sense, and work out which scene or cut is causing it.

Coaching is a lot of the same job. Someone says their knee hurts when they squat. That's where they feel it, but the cause might be their hip or how they're bracing, and you have to find it before anything gets better. A lot of coaching is dealing with feelings people don't want and can't explain yet.

Games are a bit different because players choose the feeling going in. They pick a roguelike knowing they'll lose runs, and they're fine with that as long as losing feels fair. When it doesn't, most of them just stop playing and never say why.

The dragons in Feed the Scorchpot

When we first played Feed the Scorchpot it had one dragon and ten difficulty levels. Each level made things harder in a small way. Costs went up, or you skipped certain years. It worked, but it felt basic.

We suggested Jakub add more dragons, each one changing how you play, and spread the difficulty levels across all of them. That way a harder run could also be a different run.

The demo went out with the original difficulty setup and the full game launched with the dragons. It was a welcome change, the reviews have been good, and the game has sold over 25,000 copies. We can't tell you how much of that came from the dragons.

If you're working with us

We sign a game when the premise and the foundation are already there. After that, a lot of what we do is notes like the dragons one. Some are small, like a menu that gets in the way. Some are bigger, like a progression system, or on Abelina, the story.

Most of this happens before a game comes out, so there's rarely a before and after to point to. Scorchpot is one of the few times there was.
